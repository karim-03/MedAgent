# Angina (Chest Pain)

Angina, also known as angina pectoris, is chest pain or discomfort that
occurs when part of the heart muscle does not get enough oxygen-rich
blood. It is a common symptom of coronary heart disease, which develops
when the arteries of the heart become partially or totally blocked.

Angina pain can feel like pressure, squeezing, tightness, or burning in
the chest. The discomfort can also spread to the shoulders, arms, neck,
jaw, or back, just like a heart attack. Angina pain can even feel like an
upset stomach. Symptoms can be different for women and men. Angina is very
common, affecting approximately 11 million people in the United States.
There are several types of angina, and symptoms may vary depending on
which type a person has. Angina pain, also called an angina event, can
happen when the heart is working hard, or it can happen at rest.

Angina can be a warning sign of a higher risk of having a heart attack.
Chest pain that does not go away requires immediate emergency care.

## Diagnosis

To diagnose angina, a healthcare provider will ask about symptoms and may
order blood tests, take an X-ray image, and do tests such as an
electrocardiogram (ECG) or an exercise stress test to find out how well
the heart is working. With some types of angina, emergency medical
treatment may be needed to prevent or treat a heart attack.

## Causes

Angina is not itself a disease, but a symptom of an underlying heart
problem. Coronary artery disease — plaque building up inside the large
arteries that supply blood to the heart (atherosclerosis) — is a common
cause; the plaque narrows or blocks the arteries, reducing blood flow to
the heart muscle. Coronary microvascular disease, in which the tiny
arteries branching off the larger coronary arteries are damaged or unable
to expand as usual when the heart muscle needs more oxygen-rich blood, can
also cause angina.

## Differences by sex

Both women and men having angina or a heart attack often describe their
chest pain as crushing, or feeling like pressure, squeezing, or tightness.
In men, angina tends to worsen with physical activity and go away with
rest. Women are more likely to have angina during routine daily activities
rather than during exercise, and mental stress is more likely to trigger
angina pain in women than in men. Other symptoms more common for women
include nausea, vomiting, shortness of breath, abdominal pain, sleep
problems, tiredness, and lack of energy. Women are also more likely to
have coronary microvascular disease, which can make angina harder to
identify and cause delays in treatment.

## Management

To help manage angina, a provider may discuss heart-healthy lifestyle
changes, medicines, medical procedures, and cardiac rehabilitation.
