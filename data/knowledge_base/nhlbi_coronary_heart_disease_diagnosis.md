# Coronary Heart Disease — Diagnosis

A healthcare provider will diagnose coronary heart disease based on
symptoms, medical and family history, risk factors, and the results of
heart tests. Providers will likely screen for coronary heart disease at
regular checkups, and may use an app to assess the chance of developing
heart disease in the next 10 years or beyond.

## Screening

Screening tests and risk assessments for coronary heart disease should
start around age 20 for people without risk factors. Children with risk
factors such as obesity, low levels of physical activity, or a family
history of heart problems may need screening. To screen for coronary heart
disease, a provider may:

- Assess risk factors to evaluate the chance of developing coronary heart
  disease.
- Calculate body mass index and waist circumference to help determine
  whether a person has overweight or obesity.
- Check blood pressure to see whether it is elevated (high blood pressure,
  also called hypertension).
- Order blood tests to see whether cholesterol, triglycerides, or blood
  sugar levels indicate diabetes risk.

## Risk calculators

Providers may use a risk calculator to estimate cardiovascular risk score
— the chances of having a heart attack, having a stroke, or dying from
heart disease in the next 10 years or more. Risk assessments should be
repeated every 4 to 6 years in adults who do not have heart disease. A risk
score uses data from many people with similar risk factors: even a high
risk score does not mean something bad will definitely happen — for
example, a 10-year risk score of 15% means that, on average, 15 out of 100
people in that risk category will have a heart problem within 10 years.

## Diagnostic tests and procedures

If a person has symptoms or a family history of coronary heart disease, a
provider may order blood tests (cholesterol, triglycerides, lipoproteins,
sugar, or proteins that are signs of inflammation) as well as heart tests:

- **Electrocardiogram (ECG)**: records the strength and timing of
  electrical signals as they pass through the heart, and determines
  whether the heart's rhythm is steady or irregular.
- **Coronary calcium scan**: a type of cardiac CT scan that measures the
  amount of calcium in the walls of the coronary arteries; can help assess
  coronary heart disease risk for people who smoke or who do not have
  symptoms.
- **Stress tests**: check the heart while it is working hard and beating
  quickly, typically by walking/running on a treadmill or pedaling a
  stationary bike (or medicine, if a person cannot exercise). An ECG or
  another imaging test checks for low blood flow to the heart muscle
  during the test.
- **Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)**: detects tissue damage or
  problems with blood flow in the heart or coronary arteries; can help
  diagnose microvascular disease as well as nonobstructive or obstructive
  coronary artery disease.
- **Cardiac positron emission tomography (PET) scan**: assesses blood flow
  through the coronary blood vessels and into the heart muscle; can help
  diagnose coronary microvascular disease.
- **Coronary CT angiography**: an imaging test that looks at blood flow
  through the coronary arteries using X-ray.
- **Invasive coronary angiography**: uses contrast dye, injected via
  cardiac catheterization, to detect blockages in the coronary arteries;
  often used if other tests show a person is likely to have coronary
  artery disease.

Test results may show how much plaque is clogging the coronary arteries.
