# Heart Tests

## Electrocardiogram (ECG / EKG)

An electrocardiogram, also called an ECG or EKG, is a simple, painless
test that detects and records the heart's electrical activity. An EKG can
show how fast the heart is beating, whether the rhythm of the heartbeats
is steady or irregular, and the strength and timing of the electrical
impulses passing through each part of the heart. An EKG may be done as
part of a routine exam to screen for heart disease, and may be recorded in
a doctor's office, outpatient facility, hospital before major surgery, or
as part of stress testing.

For the test, the person lies still on a table while electrodes are
attached to the skin on the chest, arms, and legs; the electrodes connect
by wires to a machine that records the heart's electrical activity on
graph paper or a computer. An EKG has no serious risks — it does not give
off electrical charges or shocks. Used for diagnosing arrhythmias, atrial
fibrillation, cardiomyopathy, congenital heart defects, heart attack, heart
failure, and heart valve diseases.

## Holter and event monitors

Holter and event monitors are small, portable electrocardiogram devices
that record the heart's electrical activity for long periods of time
during normal daily activity. They help diagnose arrhythmias (irregular
heartbeats) and check whether treatments for them are working.

## Stress test

A stress test measures how healthy the heart is and how well it works
during physical stress. Some heart problems are easier to identify when
the heart is working hard to pump blood, such as during exercise. A
provider may recommend this test for symptoms such as shortness of
breath, chest pain, dizziness, or a rapid or irregular heartbeat. If a
problem is found, the stress test can also help choose the right treatment
plan and determine what types of physical activity are safe.

The test usually involves walking on a treadmill or riding a stationary
bicycle; if a person cannot exercise, medicine is given to make the heart
work hard and beat faster, simulating exercise. Electrodes are placed on
the chest, a blood pressure cuff on the arm, and a pulse monitor on a
finger or elsewhere; heart activity and blood pressure are measured before
the test begins. The person then gradually increases treadmill speed or
bicycle resistance until the heart reaches a target heart rate for their
age. Most stress tests include an electrocardiogram (to measure electrical
activity and heart rate) and a blood pressure monitor throughout exercise,
which typically lasts about 10 to 15 minutes. The test is stopped early if
there is any sign of a heart problem or if the person is too tired to
continue. Providers may also use echocardiography, cardiac MRI, or a
nuclear heart scan to image the heart during or right after the stress
test, showing how well blood flows through the heart and how well it pumps
when it beats.

After the test, heart activity and blood pressure are measured again to
confirm they return to normal range. Intense exercise during the test can
rarely cause chest pain or irregular heartbeats, which usually resolve
after exercise stops. Used for diagnosing angina (chest pain), arrhythmias,
coronary heart disease, heart failure, and heart valve diseases.

## Coronary angiography

Coronary angiography is a procedure that uses contrast dye (usually
iodine-based) and X-ray pictures to detect blockages in the coronary
arteries caused by plaque buildup, which prevent the heart from getting
oxygen and nutrients. It is used to diagnose heart disease or follow up on
abnormal results from an ECG or exercise stress test, and can help plan
treatment during a heart attack. The procedure is done via cardiac
catheterization: a catheter tube is inserted into a blood vessel (arm,
groin/upper thigh, or neck), guided into the coronary arteries using X-ray
imaging, and contrast dye is injected to highlight blockages. If
blockages are found, percutaneous coronary intervention (coronary balloon
angioplasty, often with a stent) may be used to improve blood flow.

## Echocardiography

Echocardiography ("echo") is a painless test using sound waves to create
moving pictures of the heart, showing its size, shape, and how well it is
pumping blood. Doppler ultrasound, a type of echo, shows how well blood
flows through the heart's chambers and valves. Stress echocardiography is
done as part of a stress test, comparing heart images before exercise and
immediately after, to see whether parts of the heart muscle work less well
when the heart rate increases — a sign that part of the heart may not be
getting enough blood or oxygen due to narrowed or blocked arteries.
